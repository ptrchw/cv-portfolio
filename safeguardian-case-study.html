Case Study 02 · Design Exploration

Ideating new design directions for a modern SafeGuardian mobile app

SafeGuardian is the official MINDEF/SAF mobile app for frontline servicemen. Its pre-login screen split weather, heat-injury (CAT) status and the duty hotline across separate views. I used Claude to explore consolidating all three into a single screen — then pushed that idea through three distinct interaction and visual directions in parallel, rather than committing to one early: SITREP, Stack Cards and Civic Editorial.

The Thesis

Committing to one direction on day one is a bet, not a decision.

Most redesign briefs jump to one visual direction and iterate from there. But three genuinely different executions of the same brief surface tradeoffs a single direction never will — before any production code gets written.

What most redesigns optimise for

  • One direction, chosen early
  • One visual language
  • Consensus by default — nothing to compare it against
  • Committed before it's really tested
  • Revisited only if it fails

What this exploration asked instead

  • Can the same core screen — weather, CAT status, the duty hotline — support three completely different visual and interaction languages without changing what it does?
  • Which register actually earns trust fastest: tactical, everyday, or official?
  • What does building three parallel executions reveal about the brief itself, before any one of them gets built for production?

These aren't merely visual-taste questions; they are matters of design range and systems judgment. This is exactly where exploring multiple credible directions — not just one polished guess — makes the difference between a good-looking mockup and a decision stakeholders can actually stand behind.

The Three Directions

Same brief, three deliberately different answers.

Each direction starts from the same constraint set — pre-login only, weather as the hero feature, CAT status folded in, the duty hotline always reachable — then diverges completely in how it expresses that.

SITREP

Tactical · glass-dark

The idea

Location, weather, CAT status and hazard alerts fused into one frosted-glass "situation report" card instead of scattered widgets.

Why it works

SAF personnel already scan sitreps under pressure — mirroring that mental model means near-zero onboarding friction.

Signature card

Recent Mobilisation — the last 5 codenamed call-ups, notified time and reporting window at a glance.

Tap the card to expand full scrollable detail

Stack Cards

Warm · everyday app

The idea

Camps and training grounds live as a physical stack of cards. A swipe flips between locations; a tap expands the front card to full detail.

Why it works

Personnel often check more than one site before moving out — every location stays one gesture away without leaving home.

Signature card

A login-gated teaser — image plus a short message pointing to Hazards reporting, safety checklist and safety records once signed in.

Tap a stacked card to open a full-detail overlay

Civic Editorial

Navy · restrained authority

The idea

A flat navy hero, editorial serif/sans pairing and hairline detail rows — the visual grammar of a government service, not a weather app.

Why it works

SafeGuardian is an official MINDEF/SAF digital service; the restrained, high-contrast layout keeps CAT alerts unambiguous when it matters most.

Signature card

A plain About panel describing the app — the credibility anchor of the set, nothing to prove, nothing to sell.

Just scroll down — detail reveals in place

Applied Design Thinking

Every register down to the token — nothing reskinned.

Each direction got its own type system, colour palette and icon set, chosen to match the register it's testing — not a shared base with different paint on top.

Honest Constraints

What this exploration deliberately doesn't do.

A concept deck like this only stays useful if its limits are stated as plainly as its ideas.

01

Pre-login only

I don't hold an NS Serviceman account, so post-login screens (hazard reporting, safety records, personal checklist) aren't part of this exploration — only what's visible before signing in.

02

No official branding

The SAF/MINDEF crest and SingPass mark are intentionally absent. This is unaffiliated concept work, not an attempt to represent an official product.

03

Illustrative data

Mobilisation codenames, camp names and weather figures are all placeholder content — built to show the pattern, not real operational information.

04

A decision tool, not a decision

None of the three is presented as "the" answer. The deck exists to make a direction easier to choose — not to pre-choose it.

Bringing It To Life

Built end-to-end with Claude, one decision at a time.

From auditing the existing pre-login screens to three fully worked interactive concepts, each step below was a real design decision, made and tested in the browser.

01

Audited what pre-login actually offered

Weather, CAT status and the duty hotline lived on three separate screens. Before designing anything, I mapped exactly what each one showed and how often it was likely to get checked.

02

Decided to consolidate, not just redesign

Rather than reskin three existing screens, the brief became one screen carrying all three jobs — weather as the anchor, CAT status folded straight in, hotline always one tap away.

03

Split into three visual languages, deliberately

Instead of picking a direction and refining it, the same consolidated screen got built three times — SITREP, Stack Cards, Civic Editorial — each a complete, working interpretation, not a variant.

04

Gave each concept its own signature moment

Instead of an identical fourth card in every version, each concept earned an interaction that only makes sense in its own register — a mobilisation log, a login-gated teaser, a plain about panel.

05

Extracted a style guide from each build

Every concept's type, colour and icon choices were pulled directly from its own live markup into a reference panel — documentation grounded in what was actually built, not a separate design file drifting from the code.

What's Shared

One constraint set, three expressions.

Every concept answers to the same functional brief. What changes is entirely how it's expressed — layout, tone, motion, colour — never what's actually on the screen.

Weather (hero) CAT status (merged in) Camp quick-select Signature card

+ a pulsating hotline icon (top-right, opens the phone dialer) and a draggable RSN Chatbot access point, present in all three — the only variable is where each concept lets you drag it.

Shared underneath

Weather defaults to the user's current location; camp chips let them check where they're due to report. CAT status, including a 7-day forecast, lives inside the same view — no separate screen to open. The hotline icon and chatbot access point are present and reachable everywhere.

Divergent on top

Layout metaphor, colour, type, icon set, and — critically — how "see more detail" works: an expanding sheet, a tap-to-overlay stack, or a plain scroll. Same information, three different physical gestures to reach it.

Interaction & Motion

Three different ways to ask for more detail.

The core gesture that reveals extra weather and CAT detail is where each concept diverges hardest — and it's deliberate: the gesture is part of the register being tested, not an afterthought.

SITREP — expanding sheet

Camp carousel Swipe (or tap the location chips) to slide between camps — the sitrep card, CAT badge and hazard copy all sync to the new location.
Draggable bottom sheet The card at the bottom of the screen drags up through three snap points — collapsed, resting, fully expanded — to reveal scrollable detail.
Pulsating hotline icon Top-right, animating continuously — tap opens the phone's native dialer directly.

Stack Cards — tap to overlay

Physical card stack Camps sit as a stack with the next and previous cards peeking at the edges — a tap on either brings it to the front.
Front-card expand Tapping the front card opens a full-detail overlay titled with that camp's name — dense data stays off the landing view until asked for.
Draggable chatbot icon Free-floating — drag it anywhere on screen, not fixed to one corner.

Civic Editorial — plain scroll

No gesture to learn Extended forecast and CAT detail simply continue below the fold — the simplest of the three "see more" patterns, on purpose.
Hairline detail rows Conditions list as flat rows with thin dividers, echoing a government-portal information table rather than a card stack.
Edge-anchored chatbot tab Not a floating icon — a label that drags only vertically along the right edge, staying out of the way of the editorial layout.

Self-Documenting

Every concept ships with its own reference panel.

Rather than a separate design file to keep in sync, each concept's style guide sits right next to its mockup in the same deck — pulled from what's actually on screen, not a spec someone has to maintain by hand.

01
Typography

Display, body and CTA-label roles, named and sized exactly as used — not a generic type scale.

02
Colour palette

Primary, ink, muted and border tokens, with exact hex values for each.

03
CAT status colours

A dedicated three-step scale — Normal, Monitor, Alert — consistent in meaning across all three concepts even where the palette differs.

04
Icon set

Which library each concept draws from — Tabler, Lucide, or a deliberate mix — and why.

05
Surface & radius

Card style, button radius and other structural conventions, from frosted glass to hairline rows.

06
Font-size usage

Every size actually in use, listed hero-to-caption, so nothing downstream gets eyeballed.

How These Get Used

Three directions, one decision point.

The deck isn't the deliverable — the decision it enables is. Each concept is complete enough to react to, so the conversation can be about real tradeoffs, not about filling in the gaps of an unfinished idea.

Capability Demonstrated

What this exercise proves.

Design range

Three distinct, coherent design languages for one brief — not a palette swap three times over. Different type systems, icon sets, and interaction grammar each.

Rationale, not just visuals

Every concept ships with a written "why" — the idea, why it works, and what it's worth testing — not just a pretty screen.

Build capability

Functioning interactions, not click-through mockups — draggable carousels, snap-point sheets, tap-to-overlay cards, live CAT-status logic.

Systems thinking

Each concept's screen extracted into a documented, reusable token panel — the leap from "a nice mockup" to something a team could actually build from.

Judgment about scope

Knowing what to leave out — no post-login screens, no official branding — kept the exercise honest about what it actually is.

Decision-ready output

Three finished concepts, not one polished guess — built so a real conversation about direction can happen before engineering time gets spent.

Roadmap

Where it goes next.

Now

Three complete pre-login concepts, each with real interactions and its own style guide, ready for review.

Next

A stakeholder walkthrough — reacting to real interactions, not screenshots or a slide deck.

Then

Converge on one direction — or a deliberate blend of elements pulled from more than one concept.

Later

Extend the chosen direction into post-login screens, once genuine account access makes that possible.

SafeGuardian's pre-login screen isn't broken today — it's just split across three places that could comfortably be one. This exercise doesn't claim to know the right answer; it deliberately built three credible ones, so a real decision could rest on working interactions rather than a single early guess.

What it demonstrates is a repeatable workflow: three independently complete concepts → a written rationale for each → a documented style guide for each → one deck built to be compared directly — carried out end-to-end through AI-assisted, vibe-coded development alongside Claude.

Let's Talk

Explore the design directions first. Commit to the UI second.

An existing product deserves more than one idea of what it could become. Three fully-formed design directions made the trade-offs visible and gave stakeholders something real to react to — so the UI that gets built is a decision, not a default.
